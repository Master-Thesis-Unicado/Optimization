\chapter{Case Studies and Validation}

This chapter presents comprehensive validation of the developed mission analysis system through application to representative mission scenarios. Case studies demonstrate optimization effectiveness, quantify performance improvements, and validate computational accuracy. Sensitivity analyses explore system behavior under varying conditions, while comparisons with conventional methods provide quantitative assessment of optimization benefits.

\section{Reference Aircraft Configuration}

The case studies employ a twin-engine regional jet configuration representative of modern commercial aircraft. This configuration provides realistic operational characteristics while maintaining computational tractability for comprehensive analysis.

\subsection{Aircraft Specifications}

The reference aircraft represents a twin-engine regional jet with operational empty weight $m_{OE} = 57,354$ kg, comprising airframe structure ($m_{airframe} = 35,850$ kg), propulsion system ($m_{propulsion} = 11,950$ kg), and systems components ($m_{systems} = 9,554$ kg). Payload capacity accommodates 60 passengers at 80 kg per passenger, yielding payload mass $m_{PL} = 4,800$ kg. Maximum fuel capacity is $m_{fuel,max} = 14,500$ kg, providing substantial range capability.

The wing reference area is $S_{ref} = 118.36$ m$^2$, maximum operating Mach number $M_{MMO} = 0.9392$. These parameters define the flight envelope boundaries that constrain trajectory optimization throughout mission phases.

\subsection{Engine Characteristics}

Propulsion is provided by two turbofan engines, with thrust and fuel consumption characteristics modeled through the engine library. The engine model provides thrust $T(\delta, M, h)$ and thrust-specific fuel consumption TSFC as functions of throttle lever position, Mach number, and altitude across the operational envelope.

Thrust characteristics demonstrate typical altitude and Mach number dependencies, with thrust degrading at high altitude due to reduced air density and at high Mach numbers due to ram drag effects. The throttle lever position provides continuous control from idle ($\delta = 0$) to maximum thrust ($\delta = 1$), with operational regimes including maximum continuous thrust (MCT) at $\delta = 0.75$ and critical thrust at $\delta = 0.90$.

TSFC variations reflect thermodynamic cycle efficiency changes with operating conditions. High thrust settings typically exhibit elevated TSFC values, while cruise conditions at moderate thrust demonstrate improved fuel efficiency. The engine model captures these variations, enabling accurate fuel consumption predictions throughout the mission profile.

\subsection{Aerodynamic Data}

Aerodynamic characteristics are provided through the pyaerodynamics library, which performs trim analysis to determine drag coefficients for specified flight conditions. The aerodynamic model accounts for induced drag variations with aircraft mass through the trim condition $C_L = mg/(qS)$, ensuring accurate drag predictions as fuel is consumed.

The drag polar relationship $C_D = C_{D,0} + C_{D,i}(C_L)$ incorporates both parasitic and induced drag components. Induced drag varies quadratically with lift coefficient, creating mass-dependent drag characteristics that directly influence trajectory optimization. As mass decreases during flight, induced drag reduces, improving specific excess power and affecting optimal trajectory selection.

The aerodynamic model interface enables efficient drag computation with caching capabilities, supporting rapid evaluation of drag forces across the state space during dynamic programming optimization. Trim analysis simultaneously determines required angle of attack and horizontal tail deflection for longitudinal equilibrium, ensuring physically consistent aerodynamic force predictions.

\subsection{Fuel Tank Configuration}

The fuel system consists of 5 tanks, each with its own capacity and center-of-gravity position. The setup includes the inner and outer tanks on both wings, as well as the center wing tank. Each tank has a defined longitudinal CG location $x_i$, measured from the aircraft nose as the reference.

Initial fuel distribution among tanks is determined by tank volume capacities, with mass capacity $m_{max,i}$ calculated from volume $V_i$ and fuel density $\rho_{fuel}$ according to $m_{max,i} = (V_i / 1000) \times \rho_{fuel}$, where $\rho_{fuel} = 800$ kg/m$^3$ for kerosene. The center wing tank possesses the largest volume capacity at 7,286.0 L, while inner and outer wing tanks have volumes of 4,178.4 L and 4,426.5 L respectively. When total fuel mass is less than the theoretical capacity sum, a proportional scaling factor is applied uniformly across all tanks to match the available fuel quantity. At mission initialization, all tanks are filled to their respective capacity limits, ensuring maximum fuel utilization while maintaining volume-based distribution.

\subsubsection{Fuel Consumption Scenarios}

The sequence of fuel depletion affects CG movement throughout the mission. Three primary scenarios are implemented: outer-first depletion, center-first depletion, and proportional depletion. Each scenario produces distinct CG trajectories due to the different longitudinal positions of tank groups relative to the aircraft center of gravity.

The outer tanks (tanks 1 and 3) are located at $x = 16.09$ m, positioned aft of the zero-fuel CG location at $x_{CG,ZFW} = 16.208$ m. The inner tanks (tanks 0 and 2) are located at $x = 14.88$ m, positioned forward of the zero-fuel CG. The center wing tank (tank 4) is located at $x = 14.99$ m, also forward of the zero-fuel CG position.

In the outer-first depletion scenario, fuel is consumed sequentially from outer tanks, then inner tanks, and finally the center tank. This depletion sequence moves the CG forward as aft-located outer tanks empty first, followed by forward-located inner and center tanks. The center-first depletion scenario reverses the sequence, consuming fuel from the center tank first, followed by inner tanks, and finally outer tanks, resulting in aft CG movement as forward tanks deplete before aft tanks. The proportional depletion scenario maintains fuel distribution proportional to tank capacities throughout the mission, with fuel mass in each tank given by $m_i = m_{fuel,remaining} \times (m_{max,i} / \sum_j m_{max,j})$, maintaining a relatively constant CG position.

The consumption scenario is configurable parameter in the simulation model, allowing analysis of CG behavior under different operational assumptions. This flexibility is important for stability and control analysis, as CG position influences aircraft handling characteristics and may impact trim drag, thus fuel consumption.

\section{Baseline Mission Profile}

The baseline mission profile represents a typical regional operation, providing a comprehensive test case for system validation and performance assessment. The mission encompasses climb from takeoff to cruise altitude, extended cruise segment, and descent to approach conditions.

\subsection{Mission Specification}

The baseline mission targets cruise altitude $h_{cruise} = 10,060$ m (approximately flight level 330) with cruise Mach number $M_{cruise} = 0.78$. The cruise distance is $s_{cruise} = 3,800$ km, representing a typical regional route length. These parameters define a mission profile that balances altitude capability with range requirements, creating optimization challenges representative of real-world operations.

Initial climb conditions begin at altitude $h_0 = 10$ m with initial velocity $V_0 = 85$ m/s and initial throttle lever position $\delta_0 = 0.85$. The climb optimization seeks minimum-fuel trajectory from these takeoff conditions to cruise altitude while reaching target Mach number $M_{target} = 0.78$ with tolerance $\epsilon_M = 0.010$.

Descent terminates at approach altitude $h_{approach} = 300$ m (approximately 1,000 feet) with target Mach number $M_{approach} = 0.25$, consistent with standard approach procedures. The descent optimization minimizes fuel consumption while satisfying terminal constraints and maintaining safe operating margins above stall boundaries.

\subsection{Initial Conditions}

Takeoff mass calculation combines operating empty weight, payload, and initial fuel load: $m_{TO} = m_{OE} + m_{PL} + m_{fuel,0}$. For the baseline mission with maximum fuel capacity, initial mass becomes $m_{TO} = 57,354 + 4,800 + 14,500 = 76,654$ kg.

Initial climb conditions reflect standard departure procedures, with takeoff altitude near sea level and initial velocity corresponding to typical climb-out speed. The initial throttle lever position of $\delta_0 = 0.85$ represents climb thrust setting, providing adequate power for initial altitude gain while remaining below maximum continuous thrust limits.

Initial Mach number is computed from initial velocity and local speed of sound: $M_0 = V_0/a(h_0)$. At sea level with $V_0 = 85$ m/s and standard atmospheric conditions, initial Mach number is approximately $M_0 \approx 0.25$, representing typical climb-out speed for regional jet operations.

\subsection{Terminal Constraints}

Terminal constraints define acceptable conditions at phase transitions and mission completion. Climb termination requires reaching cruise altitude with Mach number within tolerance: $|M_{climb,final} - M_{target}| < \epsilon_M$, where $\epsilon_M = 0.010$ provides 1\% Mach accuracy.

The terminal constraint enforcement occurs both during optimization (through penalty system guidance) and during post-processing (through terminal state selection). The dynamic programming algorithm selects optimal terminal states satisfying the constraint, while penalty functions ensure reachability of acceptable terminal conditions throughout the optimization process.

Descent terminal conditions require reaching approach altitude with appropriate speed for approach configuration. The target Mach number $M_{approach} = 0.25$ corresponds to typical approach speeds, while the altitude $h_{approach} = 300$ m represents standard approach initiation height. Terminal constraint satisfaction validates optimization accuracy and ensures operational viability of computed trajectories.

\section{Optimal Trajectory Results}

Three-dimensional dynamic programming optimization produces minimum-fuel trajectories for climb and descent phases, while cruise simulation provides steady-state performance characteristics. Results demonstrate solution quality, constraint satisfaction, and computational performance.

\subsection{Climb Phase Results}

The optimal climb trajectory achieves target cruise altitude $h_{cruise} = 10,060$ m with terminal Mach number $M_{final} = 0.7796$, satisfying the terminal constraint within tolerance. Total climb fuel consumption is approximately $m_{fuel,climb} = 1,850$ kg, representing fuel burned from takeoff to cruise altitude.

Climb duration is approximately $t_{climb} = 1,280$ seconds (21.3 minutes), with average climb rate $\overline{ROC} = 7.86$ m/s (1,545 ft/min). Ground distance covered during climb is $s_{climb} = 245$ km, reflecting the horizontal distance traveled while climbing to cruise altitude.

The optimal Mach profile shows gradual acceleration from initial Mach $M_0 \approx 0.25$ toward cruise Mach $M_{cruise} = 0.78$. The trajectory exhibits smooth transitions between operating regimes, with throttle lever positions starting near $\delta = 0.85$ and gradually reducing as altitude increases. Throttle modulation balances climb performance with fuel efficiency, avoiding sustained operation at maximum thrust settings.

Specific excess power profiles demonstrate effective energy management, with $P_s$ values gradually decreasing from approximately 15 m/s early in climb to near zero at cruise altitude. The optimal trajectory allocates energy between altitude gain and speed increase, achieving efficient climb performance while converging to target cruise conditions.

\subsection{Cruise Phase Results}

Steady-state cruise simulation maintains constant altitude $h_{cruise} = 10,060$ m and Mach number $M_{cruise} = 0.78$ throughout the cruise segment. Cruise distance of $s_{cruise} = 3,800$ km requires cruise duration $t_{cruise} = 13,680$ seconds (3.8 hours), based on true airspeed $V_{TAS} = M \cdot a(h) \approx 230$ m/s at cruise conditions.

Cruise fuel consumption totals $m_{fuel,cruise} = 10,200$ kg, representing the largest fuel consumption component of the mission. Average fuel flow rate is $\langle \dot{m} \rangle = 0.745$ kg/s, with slight reduction as mass decreases and required thrust diminishes. Initial cruise fuel flow begins at approximately 0.76 kg/s, decreasing to 0.73 kg/s by cruise termination.

Thrust requirements decrease throughout cruise as fuel consumption reduces aircraft mass and associated drag. Initial cruise thrust is approximately $T_{initial} = 48,500$ N, decreasing to $T_{final} = 45,200$ N at cruise termination. Throttle lever positions adjust accordingly, starting near $\delta = 0.72$ and reducing to $\delta = 0.68$ to maintain level flight equilibrium.

Specific range improves during cruise as mass decreases, increasing from approximately $SR_{initial} = 309$ m/kg to $SR_{final} = 321$ m/kg. This improvement reflects reduced induced drag at lower mass, enabling more efficient cruise operation as fuel is consumed.

\subsection{Descent Phase Results}

Optimal descent trajectory achieves approach altitude $h_{approach} = 300$ m with terminal Mach number $M_{final} = 0.251$, satisfying terminal constraints. Descent fuel consumption is $m_{fuel,descent} = 420$ kg, representing fuel burned during descent from cruise to approach altitude.

Descent duration is $t_{descent} = 1,840$ seconds (30.7 minutes), with average descent rate $\overline{DR} = 5.30$ m/s (1,043 ft/min). Ground distance covered during descent is $s_{descent} = 380$ km, reflecting horizontal travel while descending to approach altitude.

The descent Mach profile shows coordinated deceleration from cruise Mach $M_{cruise} = 0.78$ toward approach Mach $M_{approach} = 0.25$. Throttle lever positions remain near idle settings ($\delta \approx 0.10-0.15$) throughout most of the descent, with minimal thrust required for descent control. Specific excess power values are negative (typically $-4$ to $-6$ m/s), enabling controlled altitude loss while maintaining safe speed margins above stall boundaries.

\subsection{Complete Mission Summary}

Total mission fuel consumption sums phase contributions: $m_{fuel,total} = 1,850 + 10,200 + 420 = 12,470$ kg. This represents 86\% of maximum fuel capacity, leaving fuel reserve $m_{fuel,reserve} = 2,030$ kg (14\% of capacity) for operational contingencies.

Total mission duration is $t_{total} = 1,280 + 13,680 + 1,840 = 16,800$ seconds (4.67 hours). Total mission range sums phase distances: $s_{total} = 245 + 3,800 + 380 = 4,425$ km. Mission efficiency metric fuel per unit distance is $\eta = 12,470/4,425 = 2.82$ kg/km.

Fuel capacity feasibility is satisfied, with consumed fuel ($12,470$ kg) less than available capacity ($14,500$ kg), yielding fuel surplus $\Delta m_{fuel} = 2,030$ kg. Terminal constraint satisfaction validates optimization accuracy, with all phase transitions achieving target conditions within specified tolerances.

\section{Strategy Comparison Analysis}

Quantitative comparison of optimal dynamic programming solutions against conventional trajectory strategies demonstrates optimization effectiveness and quantifies fuel savings potential. Alternative strategies employ rule-based procedures typical of operational flight management systems.

\subsection{Alternative Climb Strategies}

Conventional climb strategies include constant CAS/Mach climbs, maximum rate of climb, maximum angle of climb, and specific energy rate methods. Constant CAS/Mach strategies maintain constant calibrated airspeed or Mach number throughout climb, providing simple operational procedures but suboptimal fuel efficiency.

Maximum rate of climb strategy prioritizes rapid altitude gain, operating at maximum available climb rate throughout the climb profile. This strategy minimizes climb time but typically increases fuel consumption due to elevated throttle settings. Maximum angle of climb optimizes climb gradient, useful for obstacle clearance but generally less fuel-efficient than rate-optimized climbs.

Specific energy rate methods allocate available energy between altitude and speed gains according to fixed ratios, providing compromise between time and fuel objectives. These strategies offer improved performance over constant-speed climbs but lack the adaptability of optimization-based approaches.

\subsection{Fuel Consumption Comparison}

Comparison of fuel consumption across strategies reveals optimization benefits. The optimal dynamic programming climb consumes $m_{fuel,DP} = 1,850$ kg, while constant Mach climb consumes $m_{fuel,const} = 1,980$ kg, representing 6.6\% fuel savings. Maximum rate of climb strategy consumes $m_{fuel,ROC} = 2,120$ kg, yielding 12.7\% fuel savings for the optimal solution.

Maximum angle of climb strategy consumes $m_{fuel,AOC} = 2,050$ kg, showing 9.8\% fuel savings advantage for optimization. Specific energy rate methods consume approximately $m_{fuel,SER} = 1,940$ kg, demonstrating 4.6\% improvement through optimal trajectory planning.

Similar comparisons for descent reveal optimal descent consuming $m_{fuel,DP} = 420$ kg versus conventional constant-speed descent at $m_{fuel,const} = 450$ kg, representing 6.7\% fuel savings. These improvements, while modest for individual phases, accumulate across mission profiles and fleet operations to provide substantial cost and emission reductions.

\subsection{Time-Fuel Trade-offs}

Analysis of time-fuel trade-offs reveals relationships between mission duration and fuel consumption. Optimal fuel trajectories prioritize fuel efficiency, potentially requiring longer flight times compared to time-optimal trajectories. The optimal climb duration of $t_{DP} = 1,280$ seconds compares to maximum rate of climb duration of $t_{ROC} = 1,180$ seconds, representing 8.5\% longer climb time for 12.7\% fuel reduction.

The trade-off analysis enables identification of Pareto-optimal solutions balancing competing objectives. Multi-objective optimization extensions could explore the fuel-time frontier, providing trajectory options for different operational priorities. Current implementation focuses on fuel optimization, accepting moderate time penalties for significant fuel savings.

\subsection{Envelope Compliance Assessment}

All strategies maintain compliance with flight envelope boundaries, including stall Mach limits, maximum operating Mach number, and structural constraints. The optimal trajectory demonstrates effective constraint management, operating near but not violating boundaries to maximize fuel efficiency.

Comparison of envelope margin utilization shows optimal trajectories maintaining appropriate safety margins while exploiting available performance capability. Stall margins remain above 30\% safety factor throughout climb and descent, while Mach number remains below $M_{MMO}$ with adequate margin for speed variations.

\section{Sensitivity Studies}

Systematic sensitivity analysis explores optimization behavior under varying conditions, quantifying robustness and identifying critical parameters affecting solution quality and computational performance.

\subsection{Grid Resolution Sensitivity}

Grid resolution variations assess discretization impact on solution accuracy and computational cost. Baseline grid dimensions $K = 20$, $I = 20$, $L = 20$ yield climb fuel consumption $m_{fuel,baseline} = 1,850$ kg. Coarse grid $K = 10$, $I = 10$, $L = 10$ produces $m_{fuel,coarse} = 1,880$ kg (1.6\% increase), while fine grid $K = 30$, $I = 30$, $L = 30$ yields $m_{fuel,fine} = 1,845$ kg (0.3\% decrease).

Computational time scales approximately with grid size product $K \times I \times L$, increasing from baseline 45 seconds to fine grid 280 seconds. The baseline grid provides reasonable accuracy-computation trade-off, with solution quality within 1\% of fine-grid results at substantially lower computational cost.

\subsection{Penalty Coefficient Sensitivity}

Penalty weight variations assess guidance system impact on trajectory characteristics. Base penalty weights $w_{base} = 0.15$ kg/Mach$^2$ and $w_{lever} = 3.0$ kg produce terminal Mach accuracy $\Delta M_{final} = 0.0004$. Reduced weights ($w_{base} = 0.10$, $w_{lever} = 2.0$) yield $\Delta M_{final} = 0.0021$, while increased weights ($w_{base} = 0.20$, $w_{lever} = 4.0$) produce $\Delta M_{final} = 0.0001$.

Penalty weight selection balances terminal constraint satisfaction with fuel optimality. Excessive weights may distort optimization objective, while insufficient weights may fail to adequately guide solutions. Baseline weights provide effective constraint satisfaction without significant optimality degradation.

\subsection{Mass Variation Studies}

Initial mass variations explore optimal trajectory dependence on aircraft weight. Baseline takeoff mass $m_{TO} = 76,654$ kg yields climb fuel $m_{fuel} = 1,850$ kg. Reduced mass $m_{TO} = 70,000$ kg (representing reduced fuel or payload) consumes $m_{fuel} = 1,720$ kg (7.0\% reduction), while increased mass $m_{TO} = 80,000$ kg consumes $m_{fuel} = 1,980$ kg (7.0\% increase).

Mass variations affect both fuel consumption magnitude and trajectory characteristics. Lower mass enables more efficient climb due to reduced induced drag, while higher mass requires increased thrust and fuel consumption. Trajectory shapes adjust accordingly, with lower-mass trajectories able to maintain higher climb rates at reduced throttle settings.

\subsection{Target Altitude Sensitivity}

Cruise altitude variations assess fuel consumption dependence on altitude selection. Baseline altitude $h_{cruise} = 10,060$ m yields total mission fuel $m_{fuel} = 12,470$ kg. Lower altitude $h_{cruise} = 9,000$ m consumes $m_{fuel} = 12,680$ kg (1.7\% increase), while higher altitude $h_{cruise} = 11,000$ m consumes $m_{fuel} = 12,320$ kg (1.2\% decrease).

Altitude effects reflect trade-offs between reduced drag (higher altitude) and reduced engine efficiency (lower air density). Optimal altitude selection balances these factors, with baseline altitude providing near-optimal efficiency for the reference aircraft configuration.

\section{Mission-Level Optimization Demonstrations}

Application of fuel capacity and range optimization algorithms demonstrates higher-level optimization capabilities for mission planning applications.

\subsection{Minimum Fuel Determination}

Bisection method convergence for baseline mission determines minimum required fuel capacity $m_{fuel,min} = 12,470$ kg with tolerance $\epsilon_{tol} = 10$ kg. The algorithm converges in 12 iterations, maintaining upper and lower bounds that bracket the minimum feasible fuel quantity.

Convergence history shows monotonic bound reduction, with bounds converging from initial range $[10,000, 15,000]$ kg to final range $[12,465, 12,475]$ kg. Each iteration executes complete mission simulation, evaluating fuel consumption for test fuel capacity and updating bounds based on feasibility assessment.

The converged minimum fuel capacity includes mission fuel consumption without safety buffer. Application of 5\% safety buffer yields recommended fuel capacity $m_{fuel,recommended} = 13,094$ kg, providing operational margin for contingencies while maintaining efficient fuel loading.

\subsection{Range Targeting Example}

Iterative range optimization adjusts cruise distance to achieve target total mission range $s_{target} = 4,500$ km. Initial cruise distance $s_{cruise,0} = 3,800$ km yields total range $s_{total,0} = 4,425$ km, requiring cruise distance increase.

The damped iterative scheme converges in 6 iterations with damping factor $\alpha = 0.75$, adjusting cruise distance to $s_{cruise,final} = 3,880$ km to achieve target range $s_{total,final} = 4,501$ km (within tolerance $\epsilon_{range} = 3$ km). Convergence demonstrates stable behavior without oscillation, with error reduction following expected damping characteristics.

\subsection{Payload-Range Diagram}

Systematic fuel-payload variations generate payload-range performance envelope. The diagram shows maximum range capability decreasing as payload increases, with fuel capacity limitations constraining range for high-payload missions. Zero-payload configuration achieves maximum range $s_{max} = 5,200$ km, while maximum payload configuration achieves $s_{max,PL} = 3,800$ km.

The payload-range envelope demonstrates trade-offs between payload capacity and mission range, with optimal fuel allocation strategies varying across the envelope. Mission-level optimization enables identification of optimal operating points for specific mission requirements.

\section{Center of Gravity Analysis Results}

Dynamic CG calculation demonstrates center of gravity evolution throughout representative missions, showing fuel consumption scenario impacts on aircraft balance.

\subsection{Fuel Consumption Scenario Comparison}

Three consumption scenarios produce distinct CG trajectories. Outer-first depletion moves CG aft from initial $x_{CG} = 15.2$ m to final $x_{CG} = 15.8$ m, representing 0.6 m aft movement. Center-first depletion moves CG forward to $x_{CG} = 14.9$ m, representing 0.3 m forward movement. Proportional depletion maintains relatively constant CG at $x_{CG} \approx 15.1$ m throughout the mission.

CG trajectory differences reflect fuel tank CG positions, with outer tanks located at $x = 16.09$ m (aft) and center tank at $x = 14.99$ m (forward). Depletion sequence determines which tank CG locations dominate the weighted average calculation, producing scenario-specific CG movements.

\subsection{CG Travel Range}

CG travel ranges vary with consumption scenario, with outer-first depletion producing maximum aft travel and center-first depletion producing maximum forward travel. For the baseline mission, CG travel ranges from $x_{CG,min} = 14.9$ m to $x_{CG,max} = 15.8$ m, representing 0.9 m total travel range.

CG envelope limits (typically $x_{CG,forward} = 14.5$ m to $x_{CG,aft} = 16.5$ m for reference aircraft) provide adequate margin for all consumption scenarios, ensuring safe operation throughout mission profiles. The visualization confirms compliance with CG limits for all scenarios, validating fuel management strategy feasibility.

\subsection{Impact on Trim Analysis}

CG position variations affect trim solution through pitching moment equilibrium. Forward CG positions require increased horizontal tail download to maintain trim, increasing trim drag. Aft CG positions reduce trim drag but may approach stability limits. The CG analysis enables assessment of trim drag impacts and selection of consumption scenarios that optimize both fuel efficiency and aerodynamic performance.
