The state vector is $X = (h, M, \delta)$, where $h$ is altitude, $M$ is Mach number, and $\delta$ is throttle lever position. Discretizing the continuous state space produces a finite grid:

\begin{equation}
h_k = h_0 + k \cdot \Delta h, \quad \Delta h = \frac{h_{K-1} - h_0}{K-1}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, K-1
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
M_i \in \{M_0, M_1, \ldots, M_{I-1}\}, \quad \text{uniformly spaced}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\delta_j \in \{\delta_0, \delta_1, \ldots, \delta_{L-1}\}, \quad \delta_j = \frac{j}{L-1}, \quad j = 0, 1, \ldots, L-1
\end{equation}

Here, $K$ denotes the number of altitude levels, $I$ the Mach grid points, and $L$ the throttle settings. The state space contains $K \times I \times L$ points in total, with $I \times L$ points defined at each altitude level.

For the grid $K = 30$, $I = 30$, and $L = 30$, this gives $900$ points per altitude level and $27{,}000$ points overall (see Fig.~\ref{fig:3DStateSpace}).

    \label{fig:3DStateSpace}
\end{figure}

The three-dimensional approach allows the optimizer to select the most fuel-efficient combination of Mach \(M\) and throttle \(\delta\) at each altitude.
