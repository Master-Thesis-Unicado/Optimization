\chapter{Theoretical Foundations}

This chapter establishes the mathematical and physical principles underlying aircraft trajectory optimization. The theoretical framework encompasses equations of motion, atmospheric modeling, aerodynamic relationships, and propulsion characteristics that collectively govern aircraft performance throughout the mission profile.

\section{Aircraft Equations of Motion}

Aircraft trajectory dynamics are modeled using a point-mass formulation that captures the essential physics while maintaining computational tractability. The three-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) model considers translational motion in three spatial dimensions, assuming the aircraft behaves as a point mass with orientation aligned to the velocity vector.

\subsection{Point-Mass Dynamics Formulation}

The force balance equations govern the motion of the aircraft point mass. In the vertical plane, the equilibrium of forces yields relationships between thrust $T$, drag $D$, lift $L$, and weight $W = mg$:

\begin{equation}
T - D = m\frac{dV}{dt} + mg\sin\gamma
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
L - W\cos\gamma = mV\frac{d\gamma}{dt}
\end{equation}

where $V$ represents true airspeed, $\gamma$ denotes the flight path angle, $m$ is aircraft mass, and $g$ is gravitational acceleration. For climb and descent analysis, the flight path angle $\gamma$ relates to the altitude rate through $\sin\gamma = \frac{dh}{dt}/V$.

\subsection{Energy-State Approximation}

The energy-state approximation transforms the trajectory optimization problem by combining altitude and velocity into a single energy coordinate. The total specific energy $E$ comprises potential and kinetic components:

\begin{equation}
E = gh + \frac{1}{2}V^2
\end{equation}

Differentiating with respect to time yields the energy rate:

\begin{equation}
\frac{dE}{dt} = g\frac{dh}{dt} + V\frac{dV}{dt}
\end{equation}

Substituting the force balance equations reveals that the energy rate depends on the excess power available from the propulsion system relative to the power required to overcome drag.

\subsection{Specific Excess Power}

Specific excess power $P_s$ quantifies the rate at which energy can be gained per unit weight. It emerges from the power balance equation relating thrust power, drag power, and the rate of change of potential and kinetic energy. Starting from the longitudinal force balance,
\begin{equation}
T - D = m\frac{dV}{dt} + mg\sin\gamma,
\end{equation}
using $\sin\gamma = \frac{dh/dt}{V}$ and multiplying by $V$ yields
\begin{equation}
(T-D)V = mV\frac{dV}{dt} + mg\frac{dh}{dt}.
\end{equation}
Division by $mg$ provides the specific power rate,
\begin{equation}
\frac{(T-D)V}{mg} = \frac{dh}{dt} + \frac{V}{g}\frac{dV}{dt},
\end{equation}
which equals the time derivative of $E = gh + \tfrac{1}{2}V^2$ scaled by $1/g$ and therefore defines the specific excess power:
\begin{equation}
P_s = \frac{(T-D)V}{mg} = \frac{dh}{dt} + \frac{V}{g}\frac{dV}{dt}
\end{equation}

The formulation $P_s = (T-D)V/(mg)$ shows that $P_s$ represents excess power normalized by aircraft weight, expressed in units of velocity [m/s]. This metric directly relates to climb capability: positive $P_s$ enables altitude gain, while the distribution between $\frac{dh}{dt}$ and $\frac{V}{g}\frac{dV}{dt}$ determines whether energy is allocated to climbing or accelerating.

In the implementation, this relationship is computed using the function \texttt{calculate\_specific\_excess\_power()} within the \texttt{mission\_utils} module, which evaluates $P_s = (T-D)V/(mg)$ with appropriate numerical guards to prevent division by zero for edge cases involving negligible velocity or weight.

For level flight conditions where altitude remains constant, the energy rate equation simplifies to $P_s = 0$, which requires $T = D$. During cruise simulation, this equilibrium condition determines the required thrust setting at each point along the trajectory.

\section{Atmospheric Modeling}

Accurate representation of atmospheric properties is essential for aerodynamic and propulsion calculations. The International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) model provides a standard reference for temperature, pressure, and density variations with altitude.

\subsection{ISA Layer Structure}

The ISA model partitions the atmosphere into distinct layers, each governed by specific thermodynamic relationships. The troposphere extends from sea level to 11,000 m, characterized by a linear temperature lapse rate. The tropopause layer spans from 11,000 m to 20,000 m, maintaining constant temperature. Above 20,000 m, the upper stratosphere exhibits a positive temperature gradient.

Within the troposphere ($h \leq 11,000$ m), temperature follows the linear relationship:

\begin{equation}
T(h) = T_{MSL}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma_{tropo}}{T_{MSL}}h\right)
\end{equation}

where $T_{MSL} = 288.15$ K is the sea-level reference temperature and $\gamma_{tropo} = -0.0065$ K/m is the tropospheric lapse rate. Pressure and density obey polytropic relations with polytropic index $n_{trop} = 1.235$:

\begin{equation}
p(h) = p_{MSL}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma_{tropo}}{T_{MSL}}h\right)^{\frac{n_{trop}}{n_{trop}-1}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\rho(h) = \rho_{MSL}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma_{tropo}}{T_{MSL}}h\right)^{\frac{1}{n_{trop}-1}}
\end{equation}

In the tropopause layer (11,000 m $< h \leq$ 20,000 m), temperature remains constant at $T = 216.65$ K, while pressure and density decrease exponentially:

\begin{equation}
p(h) = p_{11}\exp\left(-\frac{g}{RT_{11}}(h - 11,000)\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\rho(h) = \rho_{11}\exp\left(-\frac{g}{RT_{11}}(h - 11,000)\right)
\end{equation}

where $R = 287.05$ J/(kg·K) is the specific gas constant for air, and $p_{11}$ and $\rho_{11}$ represent the pressure and density at the tropopause boundary.

The implementation of these relationships is contained within the \texttt{Atmosphere} class in the \texttt{atmosphere.py} module. The method \texttt{calculate\_atmospheric\_properties\_meters()} evaluates these layer-specific formulations based on the altitude input, returning the triple $(T, p, \rho)$ for subsequent aerodynamic and propulsion computations.

\subsection{Thermodynamic Properties}

The speed of sound $a$ depends on temperature according to:

\begin{equation}
a = \sqrt{\gamma_{air}RT}
\end{equation}

where $\gamma_{air} = 1.4$ is the specific heat ratio for air. This relationship is implemented in the \texttt{get\_speed\_of\_sound()} method, which first retrieves the temperature profile from the ISA model before computing the acoustic velocity.

For trajectory optimization, the conversion between Mach number $M$ and true airspeed $V$ requires the speed of sound:

\begin{equation}
V = M \cdot a(h)
\end{equation}

This conversion is performed throughout the optimization process when evaluating states defined in terms of Mach number, as the aerodynamic and propulsion models typically operate in the Mach-altitude domain.

\subsection{Density Altitude Effects}

Air density variation with altitude directly impacts both aerodynamic forces and engine performance. Dynamic pressure $q$ relates density and velocity:

\begin{equation}
q = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2
\end{equation}

Since drag and lift forces scale with dynamic pressure, the reduction in density at higher altitudes requires proportionally higher true airspeeds to maintain equivalent aerodynamic forces. Similarly, engine thrust characteristics degrade with decreasing density, creating complex interactions between altitude, speed, and performance throughout the mission profile.

\section{Aerodynamic Modeling}

Aircraft aerodynamic forces are computed through trim analysis that simultaneously satisfies force and moment equilibrium conditions. The drag model incorporates induced drag variations with mass, enabling accurate representation of performance changes as fuel is consumed.

\subsection{Lift and Drag Forces}

Lift and drag forces follow the standard aerodynamic formulations:

\begin{equation}
L = C_L q S
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
D = C_D q S
\end{equation}

where $C_L$ and $C_D$ are the lift and drag coefficients, $q$ is dynamic pressure, and $S$ is the reference wing area. The coefficients depend on the trimmed flight condition, which requires solution of the longitudinal equilibrium equations.

The drag computation in the implementation proceeds through the \texttt{PyAerodynamicsWrapper.get\_drag()} method, which performs trim analysis to determine the required angle of attack and horizontal tail deflection for equilibrium at the specified Mach number, altitude, and aircraft mass. The resulting drag coefficient is then used with the computed dynamic pressure to yield the drag force $D(M, h, m)$.

\subsection{Trim Analysis and Equilibrium}

Longitudinal trim requires satisfaction of force equilibrium in the vertical direction and moment equilibrium about the center of gravity. The condition $L = W$ establishes the required lift coefficient:

\begin{equation}
C_L = \frac{W}{qS} = \frac{mg}{\frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S}
\end{equation}

Simultaneously, the pitching moment about the center of gravity must vanish. The trim solution determines the wing angle of attack $\alpha_{wing}$ and horizontal tail incidence $\eta_{HT}$ that satisfy both conditions.

The trim analysis is performed using the pyaerodynamics library interface, which employs linearized equations to efficiently compute the trim state. This approach maintains computational efficiency while preserving sufficient accuracy for mission analysis applications.

\subsection{Drag Polar and Mass Dependency}

The drag polar relationship characterizes the variation of drag coefficient with lift coefficient. The total drag comprises parasitic drag and induced drag components:

\begin{equation}
C_D = C_{D,0} + C_{D,i}(C_L)
\end{equation}

The induced drag component $C_{D,i}$ depends on lift coefficient, which in turn varies with aircraft mass through the trim condition $C_L = W/(qS)$. As mass decreases during flight due to fuel consumption, the required lift coefficient diminishes at constant dynamic pressure, reducing induced drag.

This mass-dependent drag relationship necessitates recomputation of drag at each optimization stage using the current aircraft mass. The coupling between mass evolution and aerodynamic performance is explicitly accounted for in the predictor-corrector mass integration scheme employed in the dynamic programming algorithm.

\subsection{Flight Envelope Constraints}

The feasible flight region is bounded by operational and structural limits. The maximum operating Mach number $M_{MMO}$ represents the highest Mach at which the aircraft can safely operate. Stall boundaries establish minimum speeds based on the maximum lift coefficient $C_{L,max}$ and current aircraft mass:

\begin{equation}
V_{stall} = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho S C_{L,max}}} = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho(h) S C_{L,max}}}
\end{equation}

In Mach number form, the minimum safe Mach becomes:

\begin{equation}
M_{min} = \frac{V_{stall}}{a(h)} = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho(h) S C_{L,max} a^2(h)}}
\end{equation}

The implementation computes stall Mach through the \texttt{calculate\_stall\_mach()} function in \texttt{mission\_utils.py}, which incorporates a safety margin factor $k_{safety}$ such that $M_{min} = M_{stall} \times k_{safety}$ to ensure operational clearance above the theoretical stall boundary. This safety margin is typically set to 1.3, providing a 30\% buffer above the stall speed.

\section{Propulsion System Modeling}

Turbofan engine performance is characterized by thrust generation and fuel consumption as functions of operating conditions. The engine model provides thrust and thrust-specific fuel consumption (TSFC) across the operational envelope.

\subsection{Thrust Production Mechanics}

Engine thrust depends on throttle lever position $\delta$, Mach number $M$, and altitude $h$:

\begin{equation}
T = T(\delta, M, h)
\end{equation}

The throttle lever position $\delta$ ranges from 0 (idle) to 1 (maximum thrust), providing continuous control over engine power output. At fixed Mach and altitude, thrust increases monotonically with lever position.

The implementation accesses thrust through the \texttt{EngineWrapper.thrust\_with\_lever()} method, which interfaces with the pyengine library to evaluate the engine model at the specified operating point. The method returns per-engine thrust, which is multiplied by the number of engines $N_{eng}$ to obtain total thrust:

\begin{equation}
T_{total} = N_{eng} \cdot T_{per}(\delta, M, h)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Thrust-Specific Fuel Consumption}

Fuel consumption is quantified through the thrust-specific fuel consumption parameter, defined as the fuel flow rate per unit thrust:

\begin{equation}
\text{TSFC} = \frac{\dot{m}}{T}
\end{equation}

where $\dot{m}$ is the mass flow rate of fuel [kg/s] and $T$ is thrust [N]. The fuel flow rate follows directly from:

\begin{equation}
\dot{m} = \text{TSFC} \cdot T
\end{equation}

For multi-engine configurations, the total fuel flow rate becomes:

\begin{equation}
\dot{m}_{total} = \text{TSFC} \cdot T_{per} \cdot N_{eng}
\end{equation}

TSFC varies with engine operating conditions, typically increasing at high thrust settings and changing with Mach number and altitude due to thermodynamic cycle effects. The engine model evaluates TSFC at the current operating point established during thrust computation, ensuring consistency between thrust and fuel flow calculations.

In the cost function evaluation, TSFC is retrieved after setting the engine to the desired operating point through the \texttt{tsfc\_current()} method, which accesses the TSFC value cached during the most recent thrust computation.

\subsection{Throttle Lever Dynamics}

The throttle lever position governs the engine operating regime. Low lever positions ($\delta < 0.75$) correspond to idle and cruise thrust settings suitable for extended operation. Intermediate positions ($0.75 < \delta < 0.90$) represent climb thrust regimes with time-limited operation to prevent thermal and mechanical overstress. Maximum lever positions ($\delta > 0.90$) are reserved for emergency or maximum climb scenarios with severe operational restrictions.

These operational characteristics motivate the lever penalty system incorporated into the optimization cost function, which discourages sustained operation at high throttle settings to reflect realistic engine operational constraints.

\section{Aircraft Mass and Center of Gravity}

Aircraft mass evolves continuously during flight due to fuel consumption, directly affecting aerodynamic performance and trim characteristics. The center of gravity position shifts as fuel is consumed from different tanks, influencing stability and control considerations.

\subsection{Mass Breakdown and Components}

Total aircraft mass comprises three principal components:

\begin{equation}
m_{TO} = m_{OE} + m_{PL} + m_{fuel}
\end{equation}

where $m_{OE}$ is the operating empty weight, $m_{PL}$ is payload mass, and $m_{fuel}$ is fuel mass. The operating empty weight further decomposes into airframe structure $m_{airframe}$, propulsion system $m_{propulsion}$, and systems components $m_{systems}$:

\begin{equation}
m_{OE} = m_{airframe} + m_{propulsion} + m_{systems}
\end{equation}

These mass components are specified in the aircraft configuration module, providing the foundation for initial mass calculations and mass evolution tracking throughout the mission.

\subsection{Fuel Tank Configuration}

The fuel system consists of 5 tanks, each with its own capacity and center-of-gravity position. The setup includes the inner and outer tanks on both wings, as well as the center wing tank. Each tank has a defined longitudinal CG location $x_i$, measured from the aircraft nose as the reference.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering

    \caption{Fuel Tank Configuration}
    \label{fig:FuelTankConfiguration}
\end{figure}

Initial fuel distribution among tanks is determined by tank volume capacities, with mass capacity $m_{max,i}$ calculated from volume $V_i$ and fuel density $\rho_{fuel}$ according to $m_{max,i} = (V_i / 1000) \times \rho_{fuel}$, where $\rho_{fuel} = 800$ kg/m$^3$ for kerosene. The center wing tank possesses the largest volume capacity at 7,286.0 L, while inner and outer wing tanks have volumes of 4,178.4 L and 4,426.5 L respectively. When total fuel mass is less than the theoretical capacity sum, a proportional scaling factor is applied uniformly across all tanks to match the available fuel quantity. At mission initialization, all tanks are filled to their respective capacity limits, ensuring maximum fuel utilization while maintaining volume-based distribution.

\subsection{Dynamic CG Calculation}

The longitudinal center of gravity position depends on the distribution of fuel among tanks. At any point during flight, the CG location follows from the weighted average:

\begin{equation}
x_{CG} = \frac{m_{ZFW} x_{CG,ZFW} + \sum_{i} m_{fuel,i} x_i}{m_{ZFW} + \sum_{i} m_{fuel,i}}
\end{equation}

where $m_{ZFW}$ is zero fuel weight (operating empty weight plus payload), $x_{CG,ZFW}$ is the CG position at zero fuel, $m_{fuel,i}$ is the fuel mass in tank $i$, and $x_i$ is the CG position of tank $i$.

This calculation is performed by the \texttt{FuelSystem.calculate\_cg\_x()} method in the \texttt{cg\_x\_calculation.py} module. The method first determines the fuel distribution among tanks based on the consumption scenario, then computes the weighted average CG position accounting for both the zero-fuel aircraft and the remaining fuel load.

\subsubsection{Fuel Consumption Scenarios}

The sequence of fuel depletion affects CG movement throughout the mission. Three primary scenarios are implemented: outer-first depletion, center-first depletion, and proportional depletion. Each scenario produces distinct CG trajectories due to the different longitudinal positions of tank groups relative to the aircraft center of gravity.

The outer tanks (tanks 1 and 3) are located at $x = 16.09$ m, positioned aft of the zero-fuel CG location at $x_{CG,ZFW} = 16.208$ m. The inner tanks (tanks 0 and 2) are located at $x = 14.88$ m, positioned forward of the zero-fuel CG. The center wing tank (tank 4) is located at $x = 14.99$ m, also forward of the zero-fuel CG position.

In the outer-first depletion scenario, fuel is consumed sequentially from outer tanks, then inner tanks, and finally the center tank. This depletion sequence moves the CG forward as aft-located outer tanks empty first, followed by forward-located inner and center tanks. The center-first depletion scenario reverses the sequence, consuming fuel from the center tank first, followed by inner tanks, and finally outer tanks, resulting in aft CG movement as forward tanks deplete before aft tanks. The proportional depletion scenario maintains fuel distribution proportional to tank capacities throughout the mission, with fuel mass in each tank given by $m_i = m_{fuel,remaining} \times (m_{max,i} / \sum_j m_{max,j})$, maintaining a relatively constant CG position.

The consumption scenario is configurable parameter in the simulation model, allowing analysis of CG behavior under different operational assumptions. This flexibility is important for stability and control analysis, as CG position influences aircraft handling characteristics and may impact trim drag, thus fuel consumption.
